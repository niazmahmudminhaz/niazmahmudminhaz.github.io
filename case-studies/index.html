Evidence 01 · Search performance

Google Search Console: Find the signal before choosing the fix

Search Console shows how a website appears in Google Search through queries, impressions, clicks, click-through rate and average position. It is particularly useful when an SEO problem looks broad but the real opportunity is concentrated in a smaller group of pages or queries.

The current portfolio visual displays 17,125 clicks, 420,063 impressions, a 4.05% average CTR and a 12.4 average position. Those numbers are shown as the metrics displayed in the visual. They should not be interpreted as a standalone promise of client growth or as proof of causation.

Search performance viewContext requiredSegment before acting
What I look for

Pages with useful impressions, weak click-through behaviour, emerging query themes, indexing patterns and intent mismatch.

What I do next

Segment by page, query, brand, geography and intent before deciding between on-page, technical, content or architecture work.

From observation to action

A site-wide average can hide the actual problem. A service page, blog article, local landing page and category page do not compete for search attention in the same way. I therefore connect the query pattern with the page's job. High impressions with weak clicks may justify a closer look at search-result messaging. Low visibility on an important page may point toward internal linking, indexing or search-intent issues. Overlapping query groups may suggest consolidation rather than another article.

That distinction is central to an evidence-led SEO audit. The tool reports the signal. The consultant still has to decide whether the signal matters, what may explain it, and what change is worth testing.

Decision path: identify the page or query pattern → diagnose the reason → make the smallest useful change → measure the same signal again.

Evidence 02 · Analytics

Analytics: Measure what the visitor does after search

Search visibility is only one part of SEO performance. Analytics helps examine what happens after a visitor reaches the site: which pages attract organic users, how they engage, whether they continue through the site, and whether meaningful events are being measured correctly.

I use analytics to review landing pages, acquisition, engaged sessions, engagement behaviour, user journeys and important conversion events when reliable tracking is available. The point is not to make traffic look impressive. It is to understand whether the search visitor reached the right page and whether that page helps them complete the intended task.

Custom reporting visualNot a raw GA4 screenshotMeasurement quality matters
What I compare

Organic landing pages, page purpose, visitor behaviour, important events and the relationship between search visibility and on-site outcomes.

What I avoid

Calling a traffic increase a success without checking relevance, reporting windows, tracking quality and the page's actual business role.

Read metrics in context

A service page should not be judged by the same behaviour as a glossary article. An ecommerce category has a different job from a local landing page. A fast answer can satisfy a visitor without creating many additional page views, while a high-intent visitor may move through several pages before taking a meaningful action.

That is why I connect analytics back to search intent and page purpose. A page with modest organic visibility but strong engagement may deserve better internal support or clearer intent targeting. A page with substantial traffic but weak next-step behaviour may need a stronger content path or a measurement review before more SEO work is commissioned.

The strongest conclusions come from combining Search Console with analytics, not from treating either platform as the whole story. This creates a clearer path from search query → landing page → visitor behaviour → business action.

Decision path: confirm the measurement → understand the landing page → connect behaviour to intent → improve the page or measurement setup → recheck the outcome.

How the work is structured

Turn an SEO finding into a decision

A serious SEO review is not a checklist of warnings. It is a process for deciding which findings matter, what they may mean, and what should happen next.

I begin with the site's purpose, important pages, audience and current measurement. Then I connect technical condition, search intent, content quality, internal linking, authority and business context. This keeps the work focused on the pages that can actually matter.

The same issue can have different priorities on different websites. A technical warning is important when it blocks discovery, indexing, rendering or useful user journeys. A content gap matters when a real search need is not being met. A backlink issue matters when authority and relevance are limiting important pages. The priority comes from the evidence and the website's role, not from the number of tool warnings.

Working sequence

  • Baseline: establish the current state and important URLs.
  • Diagnose: separate symptoms from the underlying problem.
  • Prioritize: weigh impact, confidence, effort and dependencies.
  • Implement: make changes with a clear purpose.
  • Measure: compare the right signals after the change.

Content and technical judgment

Where information gain actually comes from

Information gain is not extra words. It is useful information that helps the reader understand the problem or complete the task better than a generic page would.

01

Intent

Identify the question or task behind the search and the page format that best serves it.

02

Evidence

Use first-hand observations, credible data and clear context instead of unsupported claims.

03

Clarity

Explain the finding in plain language, then connect it to a practical SEO decision.

04

Usefulness

Add examples, comparisons, structure or next steps that genuinely help the visitor.

For content work, that can mean strengthening an existing service page instead of producing another article, consolidating overlapping pages, improving internal links, adding missing context, or answering a high-intent question more directly. For technical work, it can mean proving that a crawl, indexing, rendering or architecture issue affects an important URL before recommending a larger change. Good information gain is selective. It improves understanding rather than simply increasing length.

Reading common SEO patterns

The evidence usually points to a smaller problem

Good analysis narrows the problem. It does not turn every observation into a separate project.

Impressions without enough clicks

This pattern deserves a closer look at query intent, page titles, snippets and the SERP context. The answer is not automatically “rewrite every title.” The useful question is whether the page is appearing for relevant searches and whether the result message matches what the searcher expects.

Traffic without useful engagement

A traffic increase needs a relevance check. I look at the landing page, acquisition source, page purpose and meaningful events before calling the change commercially useful. A visit that reaches the wrong page is not equivalent to a qualified visit.

Strong engagement with weak visibility

This can signal an opportunity rather than a failure. The page may already satisfy visitors well but lack enough search exposure. That is where internal linking, stronger intent alignment, better topical support or technical improvements may be worth testing.

Indexing or crawl warnings

A crawler can surface many technical findings. Priority depends on whether an important page is actually affected. I focus first on discoverability, indexability, rendering, canonicalization and architecture issues that can limit meaningful URLs or create competing signals.

Backlinks without a clear role

Link quantity by itself is a weak way to judge authority work. I look at relevance, quality, target-page purpose and the wider authority gap. Where links are genuinely needed, the work should follow a white-hat SEO approach rather than a volume-first shortcut.

Content that is already “long enough” but still thin

Length does not create information gain. The better fix may be a sharper answer, stronger examples, first-hand context, better page structure, clearer internal links or consolidation with a more useful existing resource.

A publishable standard

What I need before calling an SEO result a case study

A publishable case study needs more than a screenshot. It needs enough context for another person to understand the observation without filling in the missing facts.

  • Known source: the data or observation has a defined origin.
  • Clear scope: the reader can tell what pages, queries or systems were examined.
  • Relevant comparison: the period or baseline makes the change meaningful.
  • Reasoned interpretation: the explanation separates evidence from hypothesis.
  • Honest outcome: the page states what was measured, what was learned and what remains unproven.

This standard protects the reader and improves the quality of the work itself. It also creates a better consulting conversation because the client can see exactly where the evidence ends and where professional judgment begins.

Trust and transparency

What this page will not pretend to prove

I will not present an illustrative dashboard as a client result, invent traffic or ranking gains, fabricate testimonials, or turn correlation into a guaranteed SEO outcome. When a data source is incomplete, that limitation belongs in the case study.

SEO is affected by search demand, competition, technical changes, site changes, content changes, tracking quality and changes in search results. A responsible case study does not hide those variables. It shows what can be verified, explains the most reasonable interpretation, and keeps the next step testable.

As more projects become publishable, this page can grow with stronger evidence instead of heavier marketing language. The standard remains the same: clear context, credible evidence, useful analysis and an actionable decision.
